\begin{abstract}
Additional hidden Markov states can compensate for restrictive emission
distributions without representing additional scientific regimes. Building on
the established representation of mixture components as refined states, we
prove a uniform lower bound on the observed-law Kullback--Leibler divergence
that grows linearly with sequence length. The result assumes a compact,
identifiable emission family, positive aggregate transitions and distinct
mixture components. An exact refinement removes this approximation gap.
Simulations examine state splitting and its sensitivity to numerical optimization. In tumor allele-fraction data,
five Gaussian components describe three regional allelic profiles using
fourteen fewer parameters than a five-state Gaussian model, with comparable
held-out prediction. Technical replication supports the broad profiles, while
short segments remain sensitive to preprocessing. A symmetry-aware comparator has lower BIC but mixed predictive advantages.
A parametric bootstrap does not reject the refinement constraints at the 5\% level
(p-value 0.09); independent sequencing limits the interpretation of aggregate states.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textit{Keywords:} hidden Markov model; emission misspecification;
finite mixture; relative-entropy rate; state splitting; allelic imbalance.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Hidden Markov models (HMMs) separate serial dependence from the conditional
distribution of observations through a latent state process. This decomposition
supports applications across time-series analysis, signal processing, and
ecology \citep{Rabiner1989,CappeEtAl2005,ZucchiniEtAl2016}. Fitted states are
often labelled as behaviours, regimes, phases, or classes and then become the
main scientific object reported from an analysis \citep{McClintockEtAl2020}.

That interpretation is useful but not automatic. A fitted state is also part
of a statistical approximation. If the state-dependent distribution is too
restrictive, likelihood can improve by creating states whose emissions jointly
approximate skewness, heavy tails, overdispersion, or within-regime
heterogeneity. An additional state can therefore be statistically useful
without representing an additional scientific regime. Under misspecification,
likelihood targets a best approximation in the fitted family rather than the
data-generating parameter \citep{White1982,DoucMoulines2012}.

The practical phenomenon is established in the HMM literature.
\citet{PohleEtAl2017} show that information criteria can favour state counts
that are difficult to interpret scientifically. Additional states may absorb
unmodelled structure in state-dependent distributions, while flexible
parametric, mixture, and nonparametric observation models offer alternatives
\citep{Dannemann2012,DannemannEtAl2014,LangrockEtAl2015,LangrockEtAl2018}.
The distinction matters because methods for estimating statistical HMM order
\citep{GassiatKeribin2000,GassiatRousseau2014,Lehericy2019,ZouEtAl2024,LinHuang2025}
do not imply that every selected state is a distinct scientific regime.

Mixture-emission estimation and component aggregation already have a substantial
methodological basis. \citet{VolantEtAl2014} give the component-state
representation and an EM-based merging approach. \citet{HolzmannSchwaiger2015}
characterize minimal representations and test whether states can be reduced
to mixture components without changing the observed law. We use this
established refinement construction as the starting point, not as a new
model or estimation algorithm.

Our result concerns uniform separation from a restrictive emission class.
Under compactness, identifiable mixture order and positive aggregate
transitions, every one-step true predictive density remains a positive
$L^1$ distance from every mixture with only the aggregate number of simple
components. The chain rule turns this bound into a Kullback--Leibler divergence that is
at least proportional to sequence length against every HMM in the smaller
class, without aligning hidden paths or restricting candidate transitions.
Marginal mixture identifiability already establishes non-representability.
The additional predictive bound gives uniform separation at every sequence
length and a positive relative-entropy-rate gap. The result
complements HMM likelihood and identifiability theory
\citep{Leroux1992,BickelEtAl1998,AllmanEtAl2009,GassiatEtAl2016,AlexandrovichEtAl2016}.

The empirical comparison concerns the allocation of distributional complexity
to emissions or to latent dynamics. We compare simple-emission and
mixture-emission HMMs, including pairs with the same total number of Gaussian
components. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC), held-out sequential
predictions, and decoded paths assess different aspects of this comparison.
A simulation isolates the mechanism. The application examines unphased tumor
allele fractions, for which the measurement process gives a direct reason
for paired emission components. It asks whether low- and high-fraction bands
require distinct latent transition laws. Held-out chromosomes, a technical
replicate, symmetry-aware models and an independent assay assess the resulting
regional interpretation. The Supporting Information also examines elk movement, including
its sensitivity to the emission family and the distinction between
aggregate states and density modes.

Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives the formal mechanism; Sections~\ref{sec:em}
and~\ref{sec:workflow} describe estimation and model comparison; and
Sections~\ref{sec:simulation} and~\ref{sec:genomic} illustrate the comparison in
simulation and data.

\section{Why restrictive emissions create extra states}\label{sec:mechanism}

If a scientifically meaningful regime has a heterogeneous emission distribution, several simple-emission
states can improve fit by representing its components rather than by revealing
new regimes. The formal results below give conditions under which an
aggregate-state model with richer emissions and a refined model with additional
simple-emission states generate the same observed process.

Let $\Yspace$ be the observation set, let $\mathcal B$ be its sigma-field, let
$\lambda$ be a dominating measure on $(\Yspace,\mathcal B)$, and let $\Theta$
be the emission-parameter space. All
densities below are with respect to $\lambda$. For densities $p$ and $q$, define
the Kullback--Leibler divergence and the $L^1(\lambda)$ distance by
\begin{equation*}
 D(p\Vert q)=\int_{\Yspace}p(y)\log\!\left\{\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right\}\lambda(dy),
 \qquad
 \norm{p-q}_1=\int_{\Yspace}|p(y)-q(y)|\lambda(dy).
\end{equation*}
Here, $0\log(0/q)=0$, and $D(p\Vert q)=+\infty$ if $q=0$ on a set having
positive probability under $p$. The same notation $D(P\Vert Q)$ is used for
probability laws through their densities. Let
$\Fcal=\{f_\eta:\eta\in\Theta\}$ be the simple fitted emission family. Define
the class of mixtures with at most $K$ atoms, for an integer $K\geq1$, by
\begin{equation*}
 \Mcal_K=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^K\omega_j f_{\eta_j}:
 \omega_j\geq0,\ \sum_{j=1}^K\omega_j=1,\ \eta_j\in\Theta\right\}.
\end{equation*}
Thus, the $\omega_j$ are mixing proportions. Zero weights are permitted, so
$\Mcal_K\subseteq\Mcal_{K+1}$. Let $\Hcal_K(\Fcal)$ denote the observed
process laws generated by $K$-state HMMs with emissions in $\Fcal$, arbitrary
transition matrices, and arbitrary initial distributions. For process laws $P$
and $Q$, let $P^{1:T}$ and $Q^{1:T}$ denote the respective joint laws of
$(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$ and write
\begin{equation*}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P\Vert Q)
 =\liminf_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}D(P^{1:T}\Vert Q^{1:T})
\end{equation*}
for their lower relative-entropy rate.

Fix an integer $K_0\geq1$, interpreted as the number of aggregate scientific
regimes. A superscript $0$ denotes a data-generating parameter. The
following assumptions isolate a transparent finite-mixture setting.

\begin{assumption}[Emission regularity]\label{ass:regularity}
The parameter space $\Theta$ is compact, the map $\eta\mapsto f_\eta$ is
continuous into $L^1(\lambda)$, and every $f_\eta$ is positive
$\lambda$-almost everywhere.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Identifiable mixture order]\label{ass:identifiability}
Finite mixtures from $\Fcal$ have identifiable minimal order. Two equal finite
mixtures with positive weights and pairwise distinct parameters have the same
number of components and agree up to permutation.
\end{assumption}

Classical sufficient conditions are given by \citet{Teicher1963} and
\citet{YakowitzSpragins1968}; see also \citet{McLachlanPeel2000}. The
assumptions include univariate Gaussian emissions on compact mean and variance
sets with variances bounded away from zero.

\begin{lemma}[Separation from lower-order mixtures]\label{lem:separation}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:regularity} and~\ref{ass:identifiability}, let
$R>K_0$ be an integer and let $\eta_1^0,\ldots,\eta_R^0\in\Theta$ be pairwise distinct.
For $c>0$ and
$Rc\leq1$, set
\begin{equation*}
 \Acal_c=\left\{\sum_{r=1}^R\omega_r f_{\eta_r^0}:
 \omega_r\geq c,\ \sum_{r=1}^R\omega_r=1\right\}.
\end{equation*}
Then $d_c:=\inf_{p\in\Acal_c}\inf_{q\in\Mcal_{K_0}}\norm{p-q}_1>0$, and
\begin{equation*}
 \inf_{p\in\Acal_c}\inf_{q\in\Mcal_{K_0}}D(p\Vert q)
 \geq d_c^2/2>0.
\end{equation*}
\end{lemma}

The lemma says that a mixture containing $R$ distinct atoms, each with a
non-negligible weight, cannot be approximated arbitrarily closely by only
$K_0<R$ atoms.

To see the approximation incentive directly, let $Z_t\in\{1,\ldots,K_0\}$ be
a stationary aggregate Markov chain with transition matrix
$\Gamma^0=(\gamma^0_{k\ell})$ and stationary probabilities
$\pi_k=\Pbb(Z_t=k)>0$. Here,
$\gamma^0_{k\ell}=\Pbb(Z_t=\ell\mid Z_{t-1}=k)$. Suppose the emission in state
$k$ is $g_k$, and, for an integer $m\geq1$, define
$\delta_k(m)=\inf_{q\in\Mcal_m}D(g_k\Vert q)$. Thus,
$\delta_k(m)$ is the infimum of the emission-level Kullback--Leibler loss
with at most $m$ atoms from $\Fcal$. For specified aggregate-state
emissions $q_1,\ldots,q_{K_0}$, define the aligned risk by
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal{R}(q_1,\ldots,q_{K_0})=\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}\pi_k D(g_k\Vert q_k).
\end{equation*}
This comparison fixes the law of the aggregate hidden trajectory. In the
refined representation, the within-state component is drawn independently
at each visit, conditional on the aggregate states; the emission $q_k$ can
therefore be any member of $\Mcal_{m_k}$.

\begin{proposition}[Aligned improvement by state splitting]\label{prop:aligned}
If aggregate state $k$ is represented by one emission from $\Fcal$, the infimum of the
aligned Kullback--Leibler risk is
$\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}\pi_k\delta_k(1)$. If it is represented by $m_k\geq1$
substates, where $m_k$ is the number allocated to aggregate state $k$, the infimum of the
aligned risk is $\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}\pi_k\delta_k(m_k)$. If $\sum_k\pi_k\delta_k(1)<\infty$, the second risk is
strictly smaller whenever
$\delta_{k^\star}(m_{k^\star})<\delta_{k^\star}(1)$ for at least one state.
\end{proposition}

State splitting gives a heterogeneous state several emission atoms
instead of one. The statement is only an aligned comparison because it holds
the aggregate trajectory law fixed; attainment of the infima is not required. The next construction removes that
restriction.

We recall the component-state construction of \citet{VolantEtAl2014};
see also \citet{HolzmannSchwaiger2015}. Suppose each aggregate emission is a
finite mixture with $m_k$ components and $\eta^0_{k,a}\in\Theta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:true-emissions}
 g_k(y)=\sum_{a=1}^{m_k}\omega^0_{k,a}f_{\eta^0_{k,a}}(y),
 \qquad \omega^0_{k,a}>0,\quad \sum_a\omega^0_{k,a}=1.
\end{equation}
The $\omega^0_{k,a}$ are the within-state mixing proportions.

\begin{proposition}[Exact HMM refinement]\label{prop:refinement}
Let the refined states be $(k,a)$, where $1\leq k\leq K_0$ and
$1\leq a\leq m_k$. Give state $(k,a)$ emission
$f_{\eta^0_{k,a}}$ and transition probabilities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:refined-transition}
 \Gamma^{\mathrm{ref}}_{(k,a),(\ell,b)}
 =\gamma^0_{k\ell}\omega^0_{\ell,b}.
\end{equation}
With initial probabilities $\pi_k\omega^0_{k,a}$, the refined HMM has exactly
the same observed process law as the aggregate HMM with transitions
$\Gamma^0$ and emissions in~\eqref{eq:true-emissions}.
\end{proposition}

Equation~\eqref{eq:refined-transition} retains the probability of moving from
aggregate state $k$ to $\ell$, then draws a component of the destination
emission. The refined states therefore encode within-regime mixture components,
not new aggregate dynamics.

Assume now that
$\varepsilon:=\min_{1\leq k,\ell\leq K_0}\gamma^0_{k\ell}>0$, let
$R=\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}m_k$, let
$\omega_{\min}=\min_{1\leq k\leq K_0,\,1\leq a\leq m_k}\omega^0_{k,a}$, and set
$c=\varepsilon\omega_{\min}$. Suppose $R>K_0$ and all
$\eta^0_{k,a}\in\Theta$ are pairwise distinct.

\begin{theorem}[Observed-law separation]\label{thm:separation}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:regularity} and~\ref{ass:identifiability}, the
stationary aggregate HMM with emissions~\eqref{eq:true-emissions} has observed
law $P_0^Y$. For every integer $T\geq1$ and every
$Q\in\Hcal_{K_0}(\Fcal)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-separation}
 D\{(P_0^Y)^{1:T}\Vert Q^{1:T}\}\geq T d_c^2/2.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
 \inf_{Q\in\Hcal_R(\Fcal)}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P_0^Y\Vert Q)=0,
 \qquad
 \inf_{Q\in\Hcal_{K_0}(\Fcal)}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P_0^Y\Vert Q)\geq d_c^2/2>0.
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

The constant is uniform over the candidate HMMs, but depends on the fixed
true atoms, their minimum weight, the transition bound and the compact
fitted parameter set. It need not stay bounded away from zero when distinct
atoms approach one another or these lower bounds vanish.

The first equality follows because the refined $R$-state model reproduces the
truth exactly. For the second, every one-step prediction from a $K_0$-state
simple-emission HMM belongs to $\Mcal_{K_0}$, while every true prediction
contains all $R$ atoms with weights at least $c$. The mixture separation lemma
and the chain rule for relative entropy give~\eqref{eq:finite-separation}
for every $T$, hence the positive rate gap. This
argument concerns observed predictive distributions and does not align hidden
paths.

\begin{corollary}[Enriched-emission representation]\label{cor:enriched}
If the fitted emission family is enlarged from $\Fcal$ to $\Mcal_M$, with an
integer $M\geq\max_{1\leq k\leq K_0}m_k$, then
\begin{equation*}
 \inf_{Q\in\Hcal_{K_0}(\Mcal_M)}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P_0^Y\Vert Q)=0.
\end{equation*}
\end{corollary}

Thus, a refined model with more simple-emission states and an aggregate model
with richer emissions can describe the same observations. Equality of the two
representations does not determine which latent description is scientifically
meaningful.

A likelihood-selection consequence requires an additional stochastic limit,
not just a bound on expected divergence. For example, suppose the normalized
log-likelihood ratios $T^{-1}\log\{p_0(Y_{1:T})/q(Y_{1:T})\}$ converge almost
surely, uniformly over $Q\in\Hcal_{K_0}(\Fcal)$, to limits bounded below by
$d_c^2/2$. Then the maximized log-likelihood advantage of the $R$-state class
is at least $T(d_c^2/2-o(1))$, since this class contains the truth. Any fixed
parameter-count BIC penalty difference is only of order $\log T$.
Under that additional assumption, BIC eventually prefers $R$ to $K_0$
simple-emission states. This conditional comparison neither proves the
uniform convergence assumption nor selects $R$ against every other order.

For reference, Sections~S1.1--S1.5 of the Supporting Information give proofs
of Lemma~2.3, Proposition~2.4, Proposition~2.5, Theorem~2.6, and
Corollary~2.7, respectively.

\section{Fitting the enriched-emission HMM}\label{sec:em}

Let $X_t\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ denote the fitted hidden state at time $t$. For
state $k$, let $M_k\geq1$ be the number of fitted Gaussian components and let
$\phi(y;\mu,\sigma^2)$ denote the Gaussian density at $y$ with mean $\mu$ and
variance $\sigma^2>0$. The fitted state-dependent density is
\begin{equation*}
 b_k(y)=\sum_{m=1}^{M_k}\omega_{km}
 \phi(y;\mu_{km},\sigma_{km}^2),
 \qquad \omega_{km}\geq0,\quad \sum_m\omega_{km}=1.
\end{equation*}
The quantities $\omega_{km}$ are mixing proportions within state $k$; they do
not change the state transition probabilities. The hidden chain has initial
probabilities $\alpha_k=\Pbb(X_1=k)$ and transition probabilities
$a_{k\ell}=\Pbb(X_t=\ell\mid X_{t-1}=k)$, collected in
$A=(a_{k\ell})$.

The following standard mixture-emission expectation-maximization (EM)
updates are included to specify the fitted model \citep{VolantEtAl2014}.
The algorithm alternates between state and component allocation and
closed-form parameter updates. Write
$Y_{1:T}=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$ and let $C_t\in\{1,\ldots,M_{X_t}\}$ denote the
Gaussian-component label at time $t$. A scaled forward--backward recursion gives
\begin{align*}
 \zeta_t(k)&=\Pbb(X_t=k\mid Y_{1:T}),\\
 \xi_t(k,\ell)&=\Pbb(X_{t-1}=k,X_t=\ell\mid Y_{1:T}).
\end{align*}
Here, $\zeta_t(k)$ is defined for $t=1,\ldots,T$, whereas
$\xi_t(k,\ell)$ is defined for $t=2,\ldots,T$.
The joint posterior responsibility for state $k$ and component $m$ is
\begin{equation*}
 r_t(k,m)=\Pbb(X_t=k,C_t=m\mid Y_{1:T})=\zeta_t(k)
 \frac{\omega_{km}\phi(Y_t;\mu_{km},\sigma_{km}^2)}{b_k(Y_t)}.
\end{equation*}
The M-step updates are
\begin{align*}
 \alpha_k^{\mathrm{new}}&=\zeta_1(k), &
 a_{k\ell}^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_{t=2}^T\xi_t(k,\ell)}
 {\sum_{t=2}^T\sum_j\xi_t(k,j)},\\
 \omega_{km}^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_t r_t(k,m)}{\sum_t\zeta_t(k)}, &
 \mu_{km}^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_t r_t(k,m)Y_t}{\sum_t r_t(k,m)},\\
 (\sigma_{km}^2)^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_t r_t(k,m)(Y_t-\mu_{km}^{\mathrm{new}})^2}
 {\sum_t r_t(k,m)}.
\end{align*}
In these updates, sums over $t$ without displayed limits run from $1$ to $T$,
and $\sum_j$ runs over the $K$ fitted states.
For independent sequences with common parameters, the forward--backward
recursion resets at each sequence start. The initial-probability update is
the average of the state responsibilities at those starts; transition sums
exclude boundaries, while emission sums pool all observations. Numerical
pseudocounts and variance constraints are specified in SI S2--S3.
In computation, a small lower bound on $\sigma_{km}$ prevents likelihood
degeneracy. Multiple starts are needed because mixture-HMM likelihoods are
multimodal. For a sequence of length $T$, BIC is
$-2\ell(\widehat\theta)+p\log T$, where $\widehat\theta$ is the maximum
likelihood estimate, $\ell(\widehat\theta)$ is the maximized log-likelihood,
and the number $p$ of free parameters is
\begin{equation*}
 (K-1)+K(K-1)+\sum_{k=1}^K\{(M_k-1)+2M_k\}.
\end{equation*}
The three terms count initial probabilities, transition probabilities, and
state-specific mixture parameters, respectively.

\section{Comparing emission and transition complexity}\label{sec:workflow}

Suppose scientific knowledge suggests $K_0$ aggregate regimes, while a
simple-emission analysis favours additional states. A $K_0$-state HMM with
$R=\sum_k M_k$ Gaussian components is an $R$-state Gaussian HMM whose
transition probabilities satisfy the refinement constraints in
Proposition~\ref{prop:refinement}, with initial probabilities
$\alpha^{\mathrm{ref}}_{(k,m)}=\alpha_k\omega_{km}$. Comparing it with an unrestricted
$R$-state model holds the emission component count fixed and changes the
latent dynamics. For example, two aggregate states with component counts
$(2,1)$ have 10 free parameters, compared with 14 for three Gaussian states;
counts $(2,2)$ give 13, compared with 23 for four Gaussian states. A comparison
of two states with four components against three Gaussian states changes both
the component count and the transition structure.

The factorization requires both equal transition rows within each source
group and destination-component proportions that do not depend on the source
state. Equal rows or a low matrix rank alone do not establish the full
constraint \citep{HolzmannSchwaiger2015}. Here the groupings are specified
from the emission mechanism. The state-reduction likelihood-ratio test of
\citet{HolzmannSchwaiger2015} addresses these constraints. Under their
regularity conditions and stationary initial law, reducing $k$ states to $r$
groups gives $k^2-2k-r^2+2r$ reference degrees of freedom, hence 12 for
$k=5$ and $r=3$. Our genomic fits instead estimate a common initial law from
two independent sequences. The information about this initial law does not
grow as it does for transition parameters when only sequence lengths grow;
adding two initial-probability parameters therefore does not by itself justify
a chi-squared law with 14 degrees of freedom. We use a parametric bootstrap
under the fitted constrained model, retaining the observed sequence lengths,
the same screening and refinement settings for observed and simulated data
(SI S3.2), and starts at the exact refinement. This calibration is conditional on the fitted model.

BIC summarizes penalized likelihood within the specified model classes.
It is not a test of the number of biological regimes. In particular, mixture
singularities and transition estimates near the boundary limit a regular
asymptotic interpretation of its penalty. An unrestricted refined fit must
have likelihood at least as high as the corresponding constrained fit at a
common variance bound; initialization at the exact refinement provides a
numerical check of that nesting.

Prediction is assessed separately. Parameters estimated from a training
prefix $Y_{1:n}$ are held fixed while the filter is updated using subsequent
observations. For a validation block $n+1,\ldots,n'$,  the log score is
$\sum_{t=n+1}^{n'}\log p_{\widehat\theta_n}(Y_t\mid Y_{1:t-1})$.
The probability integral transforms
$U_t=F_{\widehat\theta_n}(Y_t\mid Y_{1:t-1})$ assess the predictive
distribution, rather than only its average log density. Exact continuous
conditional distributions give uniform PIT values; fitted models need not.
Empirical PIT discrepancies and central interval coverage are descriptive
checks here, without an independence-based significance test.

Decoded sequences and state-dependent distributions connect these comparisons
to the observations. Cross-tabulations indicate when two Gaussian states map
largely to one mixture-emission state, but do not establish that the latter is
scientifically correct. States index Markov dynamics; mixture components
describe within-state distributions; modes are local maxima of a density.
These three counts need not coincide. Interpretation therefore also depends
on temporal stability, sensitivity to fitting constraints, and measurements
relevant to the scientific question.

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

We first isolate the mechanism in a controlled setting. The data-generating
process has two aggregate states, transition matrix
\begin{equation*}
 \Gamma^0=\begin{pmatrix}0.93&0.07\\0.05&0.95\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
The first-state emission was a two-component Gaussian mixture with weights
0.70 and 0.30, means $-1.20$ and $1.10$, and standard deviations 0.55 and
0.45. The second-state emission was Gaussian with mean 3.80 and standard
deviation 0.65. The process therefore
has two aggregate regimes and three distinct Gaussian atoms.

For a reference series of length 1500, we fitted Gaussian HMMs with $K=2$ and
$K=3$, and an enriched $K=2$ HMM with two Gaussian components per state. Each
model was fitted with the protocol used for the replications below. The two-state Gaussian model merged the heterogeneous
first regime and had BIC 4487.4. The three-state Gaussian model separated its
two components and had BIC 4197.3. The enriched two-state model recovered the
same within-regime structure while retaining two aggregate states and had BIC
4191.6. \Cref{fig:simulation-density} shows the directly observable emission
fit.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.78\linewidth]{simulation_density.pdf}
\caption{Observed histogram and fitted marginal densities for the reference
simulation. The two-state Gaussian model smooths over the heterogeneous first
regime. The three-state Gaussian and enriched two-state models recover its two
components.}
\label{fig:simulation-density}
\end{figure}

Across 200 fixed-seed replications at each sample size, BIC preferred the
three-state to the two-state Gaussian model in 99.5\%, 100\% and 100\% of
samples for $T=500,1500,5000$, respectively. Comparing G3 with the enriched
two-state model, BIC preferred the latter in 96.0\%, 99.0\% and 99.5\%.
Both classes contain the data-generating law. Their fitted log likelihoods
are nearly equal on average, and the mean M22-minus-G3 BIC differences
$-6.5,-7.7,-8.6$ are close to $-\log T$. The preference mainly reflects
M22's smaller parameter count (13 versus 14), rather than a substantial
improvement in fit.
Each model retains the best fit from the original random starts and
additional data-derived starts. In particular, the exact refinement of a
fitted M21 model supplies a G3 start, without using generating parameters.
Relative likelihood convergence is checked at $10^{-9}$. The more thorough
optimization reverses 39 of the 40 original G2--G3 BIC preferences for two
states; those original failures primarily reflected local maxima rather
than state-order selection. SI S2 documents the protocol and retained fits.
The enriched candidate has four components although the
data-generating distribution has three; these frequencies therefore do not
isolate the equal-component comparison of Section~\ref{sec:workflow}.
That comparison is implemented directly in the genomic analysis. Full
computational details and additional diagnostics are reported in the Supporting Information.

These frequencies are descriptive. Finite mixtures and overfitted HMMs contain
singular parameter strata, so regular-model interpretations of BIC require
caution \citep{Keribin2000,RousseauMengersen2011,GassiatRousseau2014,Watanabe2013,DrtonPlummer2017}.

\section{Chromosomal allelic imbalance}\label{sec:genomic}

\subsection{Scientific question and measurements}

We analyze HCC1143, a breast-cancer cell line (cells maintained in laboratory
culture), and its matched normal cell line HCC1143BL, using the SNP-array
data accompanying \citet{ChiangEtAl2009}. The aim is to describe chromosomal
regions with similar relative contributions of DNA inherited from the two
parents. A region with unequal contributions may produce two bands of observed
fractions \citep{OlshenEtAl2011}.

A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is a DNA position at which alternative
single-base variants occur. The two variants assayed at each SNP are labelled
A and B. A marker is heterozygous when both variants are present in the
matched normal. The array supplies signal intensities for the two alleles;
the B-allele fraction is the B signal divided by the combined A and B signals
\citep{BengtssonEtAl2010}. Approximately equal contributions give a fraction
near one half; unequal contributions produce a low or high fraction.

Haplotype phasing identifies which variants lie on the same inherited
chromosome copy.
These array measurements are unphased: the allele designated B need not
belong to the same parental chromosome at successive markers
\citep{ChenEtAl2017}. For illustration, fractions near 0.2 and 0.8 at different
markers can describe the same regional imbalance. Switching between these
bands need not mark a regional change. This is the measurement-based reason
for a mixture emission.

The statistical question is whether the low- and high-fraction bands require
separate transition laws or can be represented within regional allelic
profiles. Here a profile describes balance or a degree of imbalance, not a
cell population. DNA copy number is the total number of copies of a region;
allele fractions alone do not determine it. Equal contributions can occur
with different total copy numbers \citep{OlshenEtAl2011}.

The data are deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), series
\href{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE13372}{GSE13372},
which contains repeated Affymetrix GenomeWideSNP 6.0 array measurements. We selected the first two accession-ordered tumor--normal
replicate pairs before inspecting their processed profiles:
GSM337641/GSM337662 and GSM337642/GSM337663. CEL files store the original
probe intensities. AS-CRMAv2 converts these to allele-specific signals;
TumorBoost then uses the matched normal to reduce marker-specific distortion
of tumor fractions \citep{BengtssonEtAl2009,BengtssonEtAl2010}. We retain candidate heterozygous
markers with matched-normal fractions in $[0.3,0.7]$, finite measurements and
unique positive genomic positions. The selection rule does not use the tumor
fraction: the normal sample identifies markers informative about allelic
imbalance without selecting them on the tumor response. Each HMM observation
is one corrected tumor fraction at a retained marker. Corrected values are
not clipped at zero or one.

Chromosome 1 of the first pair supplies 20,697 markers for estimation.
Chromosomes 12--17 supply 52,294 validation markers. After the fitted
parameters and specification were frozen, chromosomes 18--22 supplied a
further 25,413 confirmation markers. Chromosomes 2--11 were not used. The
second pair provides a technical replication on chromosome 1, with 20,937
markers, including 19,727 shared with the estimation series. Technical
replication means measuring the same cell lines again, rather than sampling
additional patients. All coordinates use the hg18 human reference-genome
assembly. Each chromosome boundary and gap exceeding one megabase
(Mb, one million base pairs) resets the
chain; chromosome 1 contains two sequences. The index orders markers by
position, rather than representing equal elapsed times or equal distances.

\subsection{Models and estimation}

We fit ordinary Gaussian HMMs with two, three and five states (G2, G3 and G5).
M21 has two aggregate states with component counts $(2,1)$; M221 has three
with counts $(2,2,1)$. The latter can represent two degrees of imbalance and
an approximately balanced profile. These are specified candidate
representations, not a claim that three is the true or globally optimal
number of genomic states. The M21--G3 and M221--G5 comparisons hold the total
number of Gaussian components fixed. G5 relaxes the transition restrictions
of the five-state refinement of M221, adding 14 parameters without adding
emission components.

Each unconstrained family uses 40 random starts, supplemented by persistent
variance-regime starts for G2 and paired-band starts for the mixture models.
The eight highest likelihoods are refined to a relative likelihood tolerance
of $10^{-8}$, with a maximum of 2,500 iterations. Exact refinements and
nested duplicated-component starts check the likelihood inclusions. All
selected fits converge, and none reaches the standard-deviation floor of
0.01. Competing solutions, transition estimates and numerical checks are
reported in Supporting Information, Section S3.

A symmetry-aware comparison uses state densities
\[
 f_k(y)=\tfrac12\phi(y;\tfrac12-d_k,s_k^2)
       +\tfrac12\phi(y;\tfrac12+d_k,s_k^2),\qquad d_k\geq0.
\]
The two- and three-state versions, F2 and F3, are equivalent to folded-normal
models for $|y-1/2|$. They have four and six tied Gaussian components and
7 and 14 free parameters, respectively. F3 is a separate scientific
comparator, not the equal-component or a nested counterpart of M221. Their predictive densities are evaluated on the original
fraction scale for comparison with the other models. This incorporates known
allelic symmetry, instead of requiring an unconstrained mixture to estimate
it. We use 30 starts and refine the best six. The BIC values describe these
fitted candidates; we do not invoke a regular likelihood-ratio reference law
or a general consistency theorem for mixture order selection.

\subsection{Components and regional profiles}

M221 estimates paired component centres at $(0.185,0.805)$ for strong
imbalance and $(0.392,0.601)$ for moderate imbalance, and a single centre at
0.498 for approximate balance. The within-pair weights are close to one half.
Posterior occupations are 38.3\%, 49.2\% and 12.5\%, and self-transition
probabilities are 0.988, 0.998 and 0.963, respectively. The names describe the
fitted fraction patterns, without translating their centres into integer
copy numbers. Figure~\ref{fig:genomic-profiles} shows their spatial organization
and separates the component densities from the aggregate profiles.

G5 estimates almost the same five component distributions. Its maximized
log likelihood exceeds M221's by 9.13, whereas its BIC is 120.87 higher
(\Cref{tab:genomic-comparison}). Its low- and high-fraction states within each
imbalance pair have nearly equal transition rows: their total-variation
distances are 0.0104 and 0.0065. The five transition-matrix singular values
are approximately $1.0003,0.9951,0.9546,0.0090,0.0051$. These are descriptive
indications of the refinement structure, not evidence of exact rank three
or a test of all factorization constraints.
The positive empirical likelihood difference rules out claiming exact
equality of the fitted observed laws. A refinement likelihood-ratio comparison,
with both models refined to the same numerical tolerance, gives a statistic
of 18.26. In 199 parametric bootstrap replicates under M221, 17 statistics
are at least this large, giving the plus-one tail probability
$(17+1)/(199+1)=0.09$ (Monte Carlo standard error approximately 0.02).
No replicate violates the required likelihood nesting. At the conventional
5\% threshold this comparison does not reject the refinement constraints;
it does not establish equivalence or identify the true number of regional
profiles. Residual dependence and other departures from the fitted model
limit the calibration; SI S3.2 gives the protocol and Monte Carlo uncertainty.

The raw G5 Viterbi path has 9,123 runs, compared with 181 for M221. Much of
this difference records switches between allele-fraction bands. Matching the
Gaussian emissions and then grouping the paired G5 states gives 99.73\%
marker agreement with M221 and 194 runs. Thus a suitably aggregated ordinary
HMM recovers nearly the same regional interpretation. The mixture makes the
aggregation explicit in its transition model; it does not make that
interpretation inaccessible to an ordinary HMM.

\subsection{Prediction, replication and sensitivity}

All held-out scores use fixed chromosome-1 parameters and sequential
one-step predictive densities, with filtering restarted at each sequence
boundary. Relative to G5, M221 gains 36.25 log-score units on chromosomes
12--17 and 5.16 on chromosomes 18--22, but loses 0.99 on the technical
replicate. The corresponding differences per marker are $0.00069$,
$0.00020$ and $-0.00005$. These support similar predictive performance with
fewer parameters, not a substantial predictive improvement or a formal equivalence test.
No equivalence margin or sampling uncertainty for these score differences
is estimated. The aggregated
G5 path agrees with M221 on 99.07\% and 99.06\% of validation and confirmation
markers. M221 assignments agree on 98.08\% of markers shared between the two
technical replicates. These are comparisons within one cell line, not
independent patient-level validation.

F3 has the lowest BIC among the specified candidates, 17.4 below M221,
although its training log likelihood is 21.15 lower. Its confirmation log
score is 15.88 higher, with 14.97 of that difference arising on chromosome 20;
its validation and technical-repeat scores are respectively 7.00 and 18.93
lower. Technical-repeat assignment agreement is 98.04\%, compared with
98.08\% for M221. Its estimated band separations reproduce the same three
profiles. The symmetry constraint thus provides a parsimonious alternative,
with predictive differences that vary across the evaluated sequences.
The empirical purpose is to examine where an unrestricted HMM allocates
complexity, rather than to propose a superior copy-number caller.

Predictive calibration is also qualified. M221's central 95\% predictive
intervals cover 96.36\% of validation and 95.48\% of confirmation observations.
The respective empirical PIT distances from uniformity are 0.055 and 0.012;
Section S3 shows the histograms and residual diagnostics. Absolute residuals
retain lag-one correlations of 0.057 and 0.142. We do not interpret
near-nominal coverage as proof that all spatial dependence has been modeled.

Changing the standard-deviation floor to 0.005 or 0.02 leaves the M221
assignments unchanged, but both bounds are below the smallest fitted
standard deviation (approximately 0.054), so these are inactive constraints.
The two alternative normal-fraction filters give 98.58\% and 98.76\%
agreement at shared markers, while changing the run count from 181 to 302
and 55, respectively. Retaining every second or
fifth marker gives 97.56\% and 96.35\% agreement. However, the numbers of
runs fall to 33 and 20 under thinning. The broad profiles are substantially
more stable than the individual short segments. We therefore do not count
all 181 runs as verified chromosomal alterations or interpret the transition
probabilities as physical segment lengths.

A further check independently exchanges the B designation at each marker
with probability one half, using a fixed random seed. Without refitting, M221 retains 99.87\% of its assignments, compared
with 56.63\% for the raw G5 labels and 99.55\% for aggregated G5 labels. F3 is
exactly invariant by construction. M221 is only approximately invariant:
its estimated weights and centres do not impose exact reflection symmetry.

\subsection{Comparison with genomic segmentation and sequencing}

We compare the chromosome-1 profiles with paired parent-specific circular
binary segmentation (PSCBS; \citealp{OlshenEtAl2011}), which partitions a
chromosome into regions with similar copy-number signals. It uses the same
arrays' total intensities and allele fractions. Its median segmented allelic-imbalance measure, evaluated
at markers assigned to the balanced, moderate and strong M221 profiles, is
0.080, 0.214 and 0.641. This supports the ordering of the profiles using an
established segmentation procedure. It is not independent validation and
uses an additional measurement that is absent from the HMM likelihood.

The published sequencing segments of \citet{ChiangEtAl2009} supply a separate
assay of relative total copy ratio, on the same hg18 coordinate system.
Between 210.542 and 221.671 Mb, 89.6\% of the 1,152 retained markers receive
the approximately balanced M221 label, while sequencing copy ratios in that
interval range from 1.438 to 2.255 (\Cref{fig:genomic-assays}). An approximately
balanced allele fraction therefore coexists with increased relative DNA
dosage. The low- and high-fraction bands on either side instead form a
sustained imbalance profile. This example makes the inferential distinction
concrete: an allele-fraction component is not an additional regional regime,
and a balanced regional profile does not establish a diploid copy number
(two copies in total).
The sequencing estimates corroborate this distinction; they do not provide
error-free labels of allelic balance or establish segmentation accuracy.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The theoretical and empirical results clarify why additional HMM states can be
both useful and scientifically misleading. They are useful when they improve
the approximation of a restrictive observation model. They are misleading
when approximation components are labelled as new regimes without testing
plausible emission alternatives.

The equal-component comparisons connect the empirical models to the exact
refinement result. Constraining the refined transition matrix groups emission
components into aggregate regimes; relaxing those constraints allows distinct
component-level persistence. In the genomic analysis, nearly identical
component distributions yield different raw state counts but very similar
regional profiles after aggregation. The reduction from five ordinary states
to three mixture states is supported by near-equality of paired transition
rows, comparable held-out prediction and the allele-measurement mechanism.
The model-conditional bootstrap comparison does not reject the refinement
constraints at 5\%, but this nonrejection is not an equivalence result.
The symmetry-aware model has a lower BIC and mixed predictive differences
across held-out sequences. These results illustrate the theoretical
distinction without establishing a uniformly preferable genomic model. Decoded paths describe fitted partitions rather than an automatic
state-merging rule.

Several limitations are important. Extra states can correct emissions,
transitions, missing covariates, temporal resolution, or multiple mechanisms at
once. Equality or near-equality of observed-law representations cannot identify
a unique latent ontology. Domain knowledge, robustness to covariates and
dependence structures, and scientific validation are therefore necessary
\citep{PohleEtAl2017,McClintockEtAl2020}. The theorem does not prove finite-sample recovery or consistency of BIC
selection. It is a one-way warning: under its assumptions, the aggregate-state simple-emission model is necessarily
misspecified.

The exact result assumes finite mixtures and a positive aggregate transition
matrix. Heavy-tailed or skewed emissions may require successively richer
approximations rather than a finite exact refinement. Structural zeros in the
transition matrix would require blockwise accessibility conditions instead of
the uniform one-step bound used here.

The genomic analysis concerns one cell line and fixed candidate families.
Chromosomes and technical repeats do not substitute for biological replication.
Unequal marker spacing, residual dependence and sensitivity of short runs
limit fine-scale segmentation. The independent sequencing assay measures total
copy ratio, not the latent allelic profiles, so it cannot establish their true
number or classification accuracy. The supplementary elk analyses additionally
show that a favorable Gaussian comparison can weaken under more appropriate
emission distributions or temporal validation. Neither the theorem nor these
examples identify a unique set of scientific regimes.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Emission misspecification can make additional hidden states valuable for
observed-law approximation even when the scientific process has fewer
aggregate regimes. Exact refinement and predictive mixture separation explain
the mechanism. Comparing simple additional-state models with enriched-emission
models at equal component count distinguishes additional transition freedom
from additional emission flexibility. In the genomic data, this comparison
recovers interpretable regional allelic profiles with nearly unchanged
prediction and fewer transition parameters. Scientific interpretation requires
evidence beyond the number of fitted states or density modes.
