\begin{abstract}
Additional states in a hidden Markov model can represent scientific regimes,
but they can also compensate for restrictive state-dependent distributions.
We formalize the latter mechanism. When aggregate-state emissions are
identifiable finite mixtures, a refined model with simple emissions reproduces
the observed process exactly, whereas a model with only the aggregate number
of simple-emission states remains separated from the truth in relative-entropy
rate. This result motivates an applied diagnostic workflow that compares a
simple-emission model, the same model with an extra state, and a model with the
original state count but richer emissions. One-step predictive calibration and
decoded-state mappings complement information criteria. Simulations recover
the predicted state-splitting mechanism. In daily movement data from four
Canadian elk, a third Gaussian state improves on two Gaussian states, but a
two-state Gaussian-mixture model is competitive for all animals and preferred
by BIC for three. The findings show how practitioners can distinguish useful
emission corrections from evidence for additional scientific regimes.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textit{Keywords:} hidden Markov model; emission misspecification;
finite mixture; relative-entropy rate; state splitting; movement ecology.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Hidden Markov models (HMMs) separate serial dependence from the conditional
distribution of observations through a latent state process. This decomposition
supports applications across time-series analysis, signal processing, and
ecology \citep{Rabiner1989,CappeEtAl2005,ZucchiniEtAl2016}. Fitted states are
often labelled as behaviours, regimes, phases, or classes and then become the
main scientific object reported from an analysis \citep{McClintockEtAl2020}.

That interpretation is useful but not automatic. A fitted state is also part
of a statistical approximation. If the state-dependent distribution is too
restrictive, likelihood can improve by creating states whose emissions jointly
approximate skewness, heavy tails, overdispersion, or within-regime
heterogeneity. An additional state can therefore be statistically useful
without representing an additional scientific regime. Under misspecification,
likelihood targets a best approximation in the fitted family rather than the
data-generating parameter \citep{White1982,DoucMoulines2012}.

The practical phenomenon is established in the HMM literature.
\citet{PohleEtAl2017} show that information criteria can favour state counts
that are difficult to interpret scientifically. Additional states may absorb
unmodelled structure in state-dependent distributions, while flexible
parametric, mixture, and nonparametric observation models offer alternatives
\citep{Dannemann2012,DannemannEtAl2014,LangrockEtAl2015,LangrockEtAl2018}.
The distinction matters because methods for estimating statistical HMM order
\citep{GassiatKeribin2000,GassiatRousseau2014,Lehericy2019,ZouEtAl2024,LinHuang2025}
do not imply that every selected state is a distinct scientific regime.

This article makes the mechanism precise and translates it into a practical
workflow. At the theoretical level, a state-space refinement reproduces a
persistent aggregate HMM with mixture emissions exactly. Predictive mixture
geometry then shows that every model with only the aggregate number of simple
emissions remains separated from the true observed process by a positive
relative-entropy rate. The comparison is made between observed process laws,
so no alignment of candidate and true hidden trajectories is assumed. The
result complements HMM likelihood and identifiability theory
\citep{Leroux1992,BickelEtAl1998,AllmanEtAl2009,GassiatEtAl2016,AlexandrovichEtAl2016}.

For practice, we propose comparing three fits: a scientifically plausible
state count with simple emissions, an additional-state version of that model,
and the original state count with enriched emissions. The Bayesian information
criterion (BIC) is used together
with one-step predictive calibration, decoded paths, and a direct mapping
between the two latent partitions. A simulation isolates the mechanism. A
real-data analysis of four Canadian elk then shows what the comparison reveals
in a familiar movement setting. The aim is not to determine a latent ontology
from data alone, but to give practitioners tangible evidence before an extra
state receives a scientific label.

Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives the formal mechanism; Sections~\ref{sec:em}
and~\ref{sec:workflow} describe its estimation and practical diagnosis; and
Sections~\ref{sec:simulation} and~\ref{sec:elk} illustrate the comparison in
simulation and data.

\section{Why restrictive emissions create extra states}\label{sec:mechanism}

The practical message of this section is simple. If a scientifically meaningful
regime has a heterogeneous emission distribution, several simple-emission
states can improve fit by representing its components rather than by revealing
new regimes. The formal results below give conditions under which an
aggregate-state model with richer emissions and a refined model with additional
simple-emission states generate the same observed process.

Let $\Yspace$ be the observation set, let $\mathcal B$ be its sigma-field, let
$\lambda$ be a dominating measure on $(\Yspace,\mathcal B)$, and let $\Theta$
be the emission-parameter space. All
densities below are with respect to $\lambda$. For densities $p$ and $q$, define
the Kullback--Leibler divergence and the $L^1(\lambda)$ distance by
\begin{equation*}
 D(p\Vert q)=\int_{\Yspace}p(y)\log\!\left\{\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right\}\lambda(dy),
 \qquad
 \norm{p-q}_1=\int_{\Yspace}|p(y)-q(y)|\lambda(dy).
\end{equation*}
Here, $0\log(0/q)=0$, and $D(p\Vert q)=+\infty$ if $q=0$ on a set having
positive probability under $p$. The same notation $D(P\Vert Q)$ is used for
probability laws through their densities. Let
$\Fcal=\{f_\eta:\eta\in\Theta\}$ be the simple fitted emission family. Define
the class of mixtures with at most $K$ atoms, for an integer $K\geq1$, by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixture-class}
 \Mcal_K=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^K\omega_j f_{\eta_j}:
 \omega_j\geq0,\ \sum_{j=1}^K\omega_j=1,\ \eta_j\in\Theta\right\}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the $\omega_j$ are mixing proportions. Zero weights are permitted, so
$\Mcal_K\subseteq\Mcal_{K+1}$. Let $\Hcal_K(\Fcal)$ denote the observed
process laws generated by $K$-state HMMs with emissions in $\Fcal$, arbitrary
transition matrices, and arbitrary initial distributions. For process laws $P$
and $Q$, let $P^{1:T}$ and $Q^{1:T}$ denote the respective joint laws of
$(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$ and write
\begin{equation*}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P\Vert Q)
 =\liminf_{T\to\infty}T^{-1}D(P^{1:T}\Vert Q^{1:T})
\end{equation*}
for their lower relative-entropy rate.

Fix an integer $K_0\geq1$, interpreted as the number of aggregate scientific
regimes. A superscript $0$ denotes a data-generating parameter. The
following assumptions isolate a transparent finite-mixture setting.

\begin{assumption}[Emission regularity]\label{ass:regularity}
The parameter space $\Theta$ is compact, the map $\eta\mapsto f_\eta$ is
continuous into $L^1(\lambda)$, and every $f_\eta$ is positive
$\lambda$-almost everywhere.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Identifiable mixture order]\label{ass:identifiability}
Finite mixtures from $\Fcal$ have identifiable minimal order. Two equal finite
mixtures with positive weights and pairwise distinct parameters have the same
number of components and agree up to permutation.
\end{assumption}

Classical sufficient conditions are given by \citet{Teicher1963} and
\citet{YakowitzSpragins1968}; see also \citet{McLachlanPeel2000}. The
assumptions include univariate Gaussian emissions on compact mean and variance
sets with variances bounded away from zero.

\begin{lemma}[Separation from lower-order mixtures]\label{lem:separation}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:regularity} and~\ref{ass:identifiability}, let
$R>K_0$ be an integer and let $\eta_1^0,\ldots,\eta_R^0$ be pairwise distinct.
For $c>0$ and
$Rc\leq1$, set
\begin{equation*}
 \Acal_c=\left\{\sum_{r=1}^R\omega_r f_{\eta_r^0}:
 \omega_r\geq c,\ \sum_{r=1}^R\omega_r=1\right\}.
\end{equation*}
Then $d_c:=\inf_{p\in\Acal_c}\inf_{q\in\Mcal_{K_0}}\norm{p-q}_1>0$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kl-gap-mixtures}
 \inf_{p\in\Acal_c}\inf_{q\in\Mcal_{K_0}}D(p\Vert q)
 \geq d_c^2/2>0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

The lemma says that a mixture containing $R$ distinct atoms, each with a
non-negligible weight, cannot be approximated arbitrarily closely by only
$K_0<R$ atoms.

To see the approximation incentive directly, let $Z_t\in\{1,\ldots,K_0\}$ be
a stationary aggregate Markov chain with transition matrix
$\Gamma^0=(\gamma^0_{k\ell})$ and stationary probabilities
$\pi_k=\Pbb(Z_t=k)>0$. Here,
$\gamma^0_{k\ell}=\Pbb(Z_t=\ell\mid Z_{t-1}=k)$. Suppose the emission in state
$k$ is $g_k$, and, for an integer $m\geq1$, define
$\delta_k(m)=\inf_{q\in\Mcal_m}D(g_k\Vert q)$. Thus,
$\delta_k(m)$ is the smallest emission-level Kullback--Leibler loss attainable
with at most $m$ atoms from $\Fcal$.

\begin{proposition}[Aligned improvement by state splitting]\label{prop:aligned}
If aggregate state $k$ is represented by one emission from $\Fcal$, the best
aligned Kullback--Leibler risk is
$\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}\pi_k\delta_k(1)$. If it is represented by $m_k\geq1$
substates, where $m_k$ is the number allocated to aggregate state $k$, the best
aligned risk is $\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}\pi_k\delta_k(m_k)$. The second risk is
strictly smaller whenever
$\delta_{k^\star}(m_{k^\star})<\delta_{k^\star}(1)$ for at least one state.
\end{proposition}

In plain terms, splitting gives a heterogeneous state several emission atoms
instead of one. The statement is only an aligned comparison because it holds
the aggregate trajectory fixed. The next construction removes that
restriction.

Suppose each aggregate emission is a finite mixture with $m_k$ components,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:true-emissions}
 g_k(y)=\sum_{a=1}^{m_k}\omega_{k,a}f_{\eta^0_{k,a}}(y),
 \qquad \omega_{k,a}>0,\quad \sum_a\omega_{k,a}=1.
\end{equation}
The $\omega_{k,a}$ are the within-state mixing proportions.

\begin{proposition}[Exact HMM refinement]\label{prop:refinement}
Let the refined states be $(k,a)$, where $1\leq k\leq K_0$ and
$1\leq a\leq m_k$. Give state $(k,a)$ emission
$f_{\eta^0_{k,a}}$ and transition probabilities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:refined-transition}
 \Gamma^{\mathrm{ref}}_{(k,a),(\ell,b)}
 =\gamma^0_{k\ell}\omega_{\ell,b}.
\end{equation}
With initial probabilities $\pi_k\omega_{k,a}$, the refined HMM has exactly
the same observed process law as the aggregate HMM with transitions
$\Gamma^0$ and emissions in~\eqref{eq:true-emissions}.
\end{proposition}

Equation~\eqref{eq:refined-transition} retains the probability of moving from
aggregate state $k$ to $\ell$, then draws a component of the destination
emission. The refined states therefore encode within-regime mixture components,
not new aggregate dynamics.

Assume now that
$\varepsilon:=\min_{1\leq k,\ell\leq K_0}\gamma^0_{k\ell}>0$, let
$R=\sum_{k=1}^{K_0}m_k$, let
$\omega_{\min}=\min_{1\leq k\leq K_0,\,1\leq a\leq m_k}\omega_{k,a}$, and set
$c=\varepsilon\omega_{\min}$. Suppose $R>K_0$ and all
$\eta^0_{k,a}$ are pairwise distinct.

\begin{theorem}[Observed-law separation]\label{thm:separation}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:regularity} and~\ref{ass:identifiability}, the
stationary aggregate HMM with emissions~\eqref{eq:true-emissions} has observed
law $P_0^Y$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main-separation}
 \inf_{Q\in\Hcal_R(\Fcal)}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P_0^Y\Vert Q)=0,
 \qquad
 \inf_{Q\in\Hcal_{K_0}(\Fcal)}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P_0^Y\Vert Q)\geq d_c^2/2>0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The first equality follows because the refined $R$-state model reproduces the
truth exactly. For the second, every one-step prediction from a $K_0$-state
simple-emission HMM belongs to $\Mcal_{K_0}$, while every true prediction
contains all $R$ atoms with weights at least $c$. The mixture separation lemma
and the chain rule for relative entropy then give the positive rate gap. This
argument concerns observed predictive distributions and does not align hidden
paths.

\begin{corollary}[Enriched-emission representation]\label{cor:enriched}
If the fitted emission family is enlarged from $\Fcal$ to $\Mcal_M$, with an
integer $M\geq\max_{1\leq k\leq K_0}m_k$, then
\begin{equation*}
 \inf_{Q\in\Hcal_{K_0}(\Mcal_M)}
 \underline D^{\mathrm{rate}}(P_0^Y\Vert Q)=0.
\end{equation*}
\end{corollary}

Thus, a refined model with more simple-emission states and an aggregate model
with richer emissions can describe the same observations. Equality of the two
representations does not determine which latent description is scientifically
meaningful.

For reference, Sections~S1.1--S1.5 of the Supporting Information give proofs
of Lemma~2.3, Proposition~2.4, Proposition~2.5, Theorem~2.6, and
Corollary~2.7, respectively.

\section{Fitting the enriched-emission HMM}\label{sec:em}

Let $X_t\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$ denote the fitted hidden state at time $t$. For
state $k$, let $M_k\geq1$ be the number of fitted Gaussian components and let
$\phi(y;\mu,\sigma^2)$ denote the Gaussian density at $y$ with mean $\mu$ and
variance $\sigma^2>0$. The fitted state-dependent density is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fitted-mixture}
 b_k(y)=\sum_{m=1}^{M_k}\omega_{km}
 \phi(y;\mu_{km},\sigma_{km}^2),
 \qquad \omega_{km}\geq0,\quad \sum_m\omega_{km}=1.
\end{equation}
The quantities $\omega_{km}$ are mixing proportions within state $k$; they do
not change the state transition probabilities. The hidden chain has initial
probabilities $\alpha_k=\Pbb(X_1=k)$ and transition probabilities
$a_{k\ell}=\Pbb(X_t=\ell\mid X_{t-1}=k)$, collected in
$A=(a_{k\ell})$.

The expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm alternates between state and
component allocation and closed-form parameter updates. Write
$Y_{1:T}=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)$ and let $C_t\in\{1,\ldots,M_{X_t}\}$ denote the
Gaussian-component label at time $t$. A scaled forward--backward recursion gives
\begin{align*}
 \zeta_t(k)&=\Pbb(X_t=k\mid Y_{1:T}),\\
 \xi_t(k,\ell)&=\Pbb(X_{t-1}=k,X_t=\ell\mid Y_{1:T}).
\end{align*}
Here, $\zeta_t(k)$ is defined for $t=1,\ldots,T$, whereas
$\xi_t(k,\ell)$ is defined for $t=2,\ldots,T$.
The joint posterior responsibility for state $k$ and component $m$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:responsibility}
 r_t(k,m)=\Pbb(X_t=k,C_t=m\mid Y_{1:T})=\zeta_t(k)
 \frac{\omega_{km}\phi(Y_t;\mu_{km},\sigma_{km}^2)}{b_k(Y_t)}.
\end{equation}
The M-step updates are
\begin{align}
 \alpha_k^{\mathrm{new}}&=\zeta_1(k), &
 a_{k\ell}^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_{t=2}^T\xi_t(k,\ell)}
 {\sum_{t=2}^T\sum_j\xi_t(k,j)},\label{eq:em-chain}\\
 \omega_{km}^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_t r_t(k,m)}{\sum_t\zeta_t(k)}, &
 \mu_{km}^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_t r_t(k,m)Y_t}{\sum_t r_t(k,m)},\label{eq:em-weight-mean}\\
 (\sigma_{km}^2)^{\mathrm{new}}&=
 \frac{\sum_t r_t(k,m)(Y_t-\mu_{km}^{\mathrm{new}})^2}
 {\sum_t r_t(k,m)}.\label{eq:em-variance}
\end{align}
In these updates, sums over $t$ without displayed limits run from $1$ to $T$,
and $\sum_j$ runs over the $K$ fitted states.
In computation, a small lower bound on $\sigma_{km}$ prevents likelihood
degeneracy. Multiple starts are needed because mixture-HMM likelihoods are
multimodal. For a sequence of length $T$, BIC is
$-2\ell(\widehat\theta)+p\log T$, where $\widehat\theta$ is the maximum
likelihood estimate, $\ell(\widehat\theta)$ is the maximized log-likelihood,
and the number $p$ of free parameters is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parameter-count}
 (K-1)+K(K-1)+\sum_{k=1}^K\{(M_k-1)+2M_k\}.
\end{equation}
The three terms count initial probabilities, transition probabilities, and
state-specific mixture parameters, respectively.

\section{A diagnostic workflow for practitioners}\label{sec:workflow}

Suppose scientific knowledge suggests $K_0$ regimes, while a simple-emission
analysis favours $K_0+1$ states. We recommend the following comparison.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Fit the simple-emission HMMs with $K_0$ and $K_0+1$ states, then fit a
$K_0$-state model with a scientifically plausible richer emission family.
\item Compare penalized fit, but do not use BIC alone as evidence for a new
scientific regime.
\item Check one-step predictive calibration. For continuous data, compute the
probability integral transform (PIT) values
$U_t=F_t(Y_t\mid Y_{1:t-1})$, where $F_t$ is the fitted one-step predictive
cumulative distribution function. A calibrated model should produce PIT values
approximately uniform on $(0,1)$.
\item Compare decoded sequences and state-dependent distributions. Cross-tabulate
the $K_0+1$-state decoding against the enriched $K_0$-state decoding. If two
simple-emission states map largely to one enriched state, they are candidates
for an emission-driven split.
\item Present the observed series, predictive diagnostics, and mapping to the
context researcher. Interpret an extra state substantively only when its
temporal and domain-specific meaning remains after plausible emission
enrichment.
\end{enumerate}

These steps do not prove that an extra state is corrective. They provide
direct, data-related evidence that is easier to discuss than a BIC difference
alone. Formal goodness-of-fit procedures, sensitivity to starting values, and
subject-matter validation remain important.

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}

We first isolate the mechanism in a controlled setting. The data-generating
process has two aggregate states, transition matrix
\begin{equation*}
 \Gamma^0=\begin{pmatrix}0.93&0.07\\0.05&0.95\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
The first-state emission was a two-component Gaussian mixture with weights
0.70 and 0.30, means $-1.20$ and $1.10$, and standard deviations 0.55 and
0.45. The second-state emission was Gaussian with mean 3.80 and standard
deviation 0.65. The process therefore
has two aggregate regimes and three distinct Gaussian atoms.

For a reference series of length 1500, we fitted Gaussian HMMs with $K=2$ and
$K=3$, and an enriched $K=2$ HMM with two Gaussian components per state. Each
model used 10 EM starts. The two-state Gaussian model merged the heterogeneous
first regime and had BIC 4487.4. The three-state Gaussian model separated its
two components and had BIC 4197.3. The enriched two-state model recovered the
same within-regime structure while retaining two aggregate states and had BIC
4191.8. \Cref{fig:simulation-density} shows the directly observable emission
fit.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.78\linewidth]{simulation_density.pdf}
\caption{Observed histogram and fitted marginal densities for the reference
simulation. The two-state Gaussian model smooths over the heterogeneous first
regime. The three-state Gaussian and enriched two-state models recover its two

\label{fig:simulation-density}
\end{figure}

Across 200 fixed-seed replications at each sample size, BIC selected the third
Gaussian state in 89.5\%, 94.5\%, and 96.0\% of samples for
$T=500,1500,5000$, respectively. When the three-state Gaussian model was
compared with the enriched two-state model, BIC preferred the latter in 94.0\%,
98.5\%, and 98.5\% of samples. Full computational details and additional
diagnostics are reported in the Supporting Information.

These frequencies are descriptive. Finite mixtures and overfitted HMMs contain
singular parameter strata, so regular-model interpretations of BIC require
caution \citep{Keribin2000,RousseauMengersen2011,GassiatRousseau2014,Watanabe2013,DrtonPlummer2017}.

\section{Canadian elk movement application}\label{sec:elk}

\subsection{Data and analysis}

We used the public \texttt{elk\_data} distributed with the R package
\texttt{moveHMM} \citep{MichelotEtAl2016}. The data contain 735 ordered GPS
locations for four elk in east-central Ontario, with 194, 159, 164, and 218
locations for elk 115, 163, 287, and 363, respectively. The animals and study
setting originate from \citet{MoralesEtAl2004}. Exact timestamps are absent
from the distributed data; following the documented use of elk 115 in
\citet{PartonBlackwell2017}, consecutive locations were treated as
approximately 24 hours apart. We calculated 193, 158, 163, and 217 daily step
lengths.

The analysis deliberately uses only step length. It is not a full ecological
reanalysis and does not assign behavioural labels. Its purpose is to show how
emission flexibility changes the interpretation of fitted state count. For
each animal separately, the response was $\log(1+\text{step length in km})$.
We fitted Gaussian HMMs with $K=2$ and $K=3$, plus an enriched $K=2$ HMM with
two Gaussian components in each state-dependent distribution. Separate fits
avoid imposing identical movement distributions across animals. Each fit used
200 deterministic-seed starts, a fitted standard-deviation lower bound of 0.05,
and the same screening and convergence rules. The models have 7, 14, and 13
free parameters, respectively.

We compared BIC and the empirical distribution of in-sample one-step PIT
values. Because the same observations are used to estimate and assess each
model, these PIT summaries are descriptive calibration diagnostics rather than
out-of-sample performance estimates. We also compared Viterbi-decoded state
sequences, meaning the most likely joint hidden-state paths, and
cross-tabulated the three Gaussian states against the two enriched-emission
states.

\subsection{Results}

The three-state Gaussian model improved substantially on the two-state
Gaussian model for every animal, with BIC reductions from 15.5 to 65.1
\Cref{tab:elk-comparison}. That result alone could be presented as evidence for
a third movement state. The enriched comparison changes the interpretation.
The two-state mixture model had lower BIC than the three-state Gaussian model
for elk 163, 287, and 363, by 10.8, 8.7, and 8.0 points. For elk 115 its BIC was
2.8 points higher, so the two representations were close on that criterion.

The enriched two-state model also had the smallest PIT distance for elk 115,
287, and 363, and was close to the three-state Gaussian model for elk 163
\Cref{fig:elk-diagnostics}. Its central 90\% predictive interval coverage was
between 0.914 and 0.931 across animals. The corresponding range was 0.914 to
0.948 for the three-state Gaussian model. Thus, reducing the number of states
after enriching the emissions did not produce a general loss of predictive
calibration.

The fitted component distributions show what the enrichment contributes beyond
a third Gaussian state. For elk 115, one enriched state combines component
medians of 2.60 and 9.38 km with weights 0.70 and 0.30. The corresponding pairs
are 0.58 and 5.26 km for elk 163, 0.11 and 3.77 km for elk 287, and 0.13 and
4.44 km for elk 363. Thus, a single aggregate state can accommodate distinct
parts of the observed step-length distribution without assigning each part a
separate state. These components are distributional features, not behavioural
categories; complete component and transition estimates are reported in the
Supporting Information.

The only near-boundary transition estimates occurred for elk 287: the two
self-transition probabilities were 1.000 and 0.989. Inference about aggregate
switching for that individual should therefore be treated with particular
caution.

The decoded-state mapping makes the mechanism tangible
\Cref{fig:elk-mapping}. For elk 115, the first two Gaussian states mapped almost
entirely to the first enriched state, while the third mapped to the second. For
elk 287, the first and third Gaussian states mapped to one enriched state and
the second mapped to the other, up to label permutation. Elk 163 showed the
same broad aggregation with partial overlap in one Gaussian state. The mapping
for elk 363 was less discrete, indicating that the two latent descriptions
should not be treated as interchangeable for that animal.

Taken together, the observation-level diagnostics support an emission-driven
explanation for at least part of the third Gaussian state. They do not establish
that elk movement has exactly two biological behaviours. Instead, they show
that the evidence for a third state is sensitive to the state-dependent
distribution and should not be presented to a context researcher as a new
behaviour on BIC alone.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The theoretical and empirical results clarify why additional HMM states can be
both useful and scientifically misleading. They are useful when they improve
the approximation of a restrictive observation model. They are misleading
when approximation components are labelled as new regimes without testing
plausible emission alternatives.

The proposed workflow makes that test directly visible. In the elk example,
three Gaussian states fit better than two, but two richer state-dependent
distributions match or improve the penalized fit for three animals and improve
the PIT distance for three. Decoded-state cross-tabulations show where the
third Gaussian partition collapses after enrichment. These displays are more
tangible than a BIC table alone, yet they remain diagnostics rather than an
automatic state-merging rule.

Several limitations are important. Extra states can correct emissions,
transitions, missing covariates, temporal resolution, or multiple mechanisms at
once. Equality or near-equality of observed-law representations cannot identify
a unique latent ontology. Domain knowledge, robustness to covariates and
dependence structures, and scientific validation are therefore necessary
\citep{PohleEtAl2017,McClintockEtAl2020}. The theorem is a one-way warning:
under its assumptions, the aggregate-state simple-emission model is necessarily
misspecified.

The exact result assumes finite mixtures and a positive aggregate transition
matrix. Heavy-tailed or skewed emissions may require successively richer
approximations rather than a finite exact refinement. Structural zeros in the
transition matrix would require blockwise accessibility conditions instead of
the uniform one-step bound used here.

The elk illustration has additional limitations. The public data lack exact
timestamps, the sample sizes per animal are modest, only step length is used,
and the PIT checks are in-sample. Direction, habitat covariates, individual
heterogeneity, and irregular observation times are outside its scope. The
analysis consequently demonstrates a diagnostic workflow, not a definitive
behavioural classification.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Emission misspecification can make additional hidden states valuable for
observed-law approximation even when the scientific process has fewer
aggregate regimes. Exact refinement and predictive mixture separation explain
the mechanism. Comparing simple additional-state models with enriched-emission
models, then examining predictive calibration and decoded-state mappings, gives
practitioners concrete evidence before assigning substantive meaning to an
extra state.
